The three rules that protect children in every game

Friends they know in real life

Only add or play with people your child has met in person. Most serious harm starts with a friendly stranger.

Never move to another app

Anyone who asks to chat on Discord, Snapchat or WhatsApp, or asks for photos, is a red flag. Report, block and tell an adult.

Telling you is always the right thing

Children often keep problems to themselves because they're afraid of losing the game or their device. Tell them they won't be in trouble, and that you won't take the game away because they told you. Stay calm, thank them, and work out the next step together. If family rules or settings need to change, talk about it together once things are calm.

What your child is actually doing

Think of Roblox as YouTube for games. Your child opens the app and picks from a huge library of games ("experiences") made by other users: companies, adults, and teenagers. One moment they're raising a pet, the next they're in an obstacle course, a role-play town, a shooter, or a horror game.

A typical 20 minutes: they join a game with 10–50 other players (often strangers, sometimes classmates), complete a loop like "collect, upgrade, collect more", get offered something to buy with Robux, then hop to another game a friend is playing. Many children also build their own games in Roblox Studio, which is where most of the learning happens.

Why they love it: it's where their friends are, there's always something new, and it's completely theirs. Being left out of the Roblox conversation at school feels like a big deal to them.

The most-played games change almost weekly. These were near the top of the charts in November:

Steal an EggReleased July 2026 and currently #1. Players steal eggs from guarded nests, hatch them into pets that earn in-game money, and protect their base from other players. It's rated Minimal (all ages), but it has large Discord fan servers for trading and giveaways. Those are off-platform spaces that Roblox's protections don't cover.
Brookhaven RPA role-play town: houses, cars, jobs, "families". Gentle on the surface, but role-play games are where inappropriate role-play by strangers tends to happen. Keep chat limited.
Adopt Me!Adopt and raise pets, then trade them. Trading means trading scams. Children regularly lose rare pets to "trust trades".
Blox FruitsAn anime-style fighting adventure with a very long grind and lots of paid boosts.
99 Nights in the ForestSurvive 99 nights in a dark forest with friends. Mildly scary, with a strong team element.
Murder Mystery 2 & RIVALSMM2: one player is secretly the murderer. RIVALS: a competitive shooter. Both have large markets for weapon skins.
Dandy's WorldA horror-style survival game that got its big "1.0" update on 20 November. Can genuinely frighten younger children.
Steal a BrainrotCollect and steal "brainrot" meme characters. This is where many of the nonsense Italian-sounding names children chant at home come from.

What's changed this year: since January, an age check (a face scan or ID) is needed to use chat. Since June, children are placed in Roblox Kids (5–8) or Roblox Select (9–15) accounts with a smaller, vetted set of games. These changes help, but they've already been bypassed. Age-verified accounts were sold online within days, and Australia's regulator still found adults able to contact children in 2026.

What's good about it

  • Creativity and coding. Roblox Studio is a real game engine, and many children's first programming happens there.
  • Social play with real-life friends after school.
  • Problem-solving and teamwork in many of the games.

The real risks

Contact from strangers and grooming Most serious

The pattern seen in court cases and regulator findings: an adult, often posing as a child, befriends a child in a game, offers Robux or gifts, then moves the chat to Discord or Snapchat, where there are fewer protections. Roblox blocks images and links in chat, which is exactly why predators try to move children elsewhere.

Spending pressure Very common

A 2026 University of Sydney study found pressure to spend in 14 of the 15 popular Roblox games it examined, often designed in ways that mislead children about the real cost: layered currencies, timed shops and paid "revives". The EU is investigating Roblox over these "dark patterns".

Unsuitable content Common

With millions of user-made games, violent, sexual or frightening content gets past moderation, sometimes in games that look child-friendly.

Scams and account theft Common

Common examples are "free Robux" sites, fake trades, and phishing links. Lost items are rarely returned.

Bullying and exclusion Common

Playground conflicts carry into games and back into school.

Reduce the risk: the five things that matter most

  1. Correct birth date on the account. Every protection depends on it.
  2. Link your own parent account so you control the settings.
  3. Chat off, or real-life friends only.
  4. Spending limit set, with no card saved on the device.
  5. Talk regularly: what are you playing, and who with?

Set up parental controls, step by step

  1. On your child's account: go to Settings › Parental Controls › Add parent, then enter your email address.
  2. Follow the email to create your own Roblox account and verify you're an adult. You may also be asked to create a Parent PIN, which lets you approve your child's requests quickly.
  3. From your own phone: Settings › Parental Controls › choose your child › Settings you manage.
  4. Content restrictions: set the maturity level (Minimal or Mild for younger children). You can also block specific games, or require your approval for new ones.
  5. Communication: turn chat off, or limit it to friends. Leave one-to-one (direct) chat off.
  6. Spending: set a monthly limit, or zero. Turn on notifications for purchases.
  7. Screen time: set a daily limit.
  8. Security: turn on 2-step verification. Review the friends list together and remove anyone your child hasn't met.
Age check: to chat, your child will be asked to do a face scan (run by a company called Persona, which Roblox says deletes images straight away) or an ID check. If you'd rather avoid this, simply leave chat off. Playing doesn't require a check.

Menu names may differ slightly by device and region. Roblox's own guide is at about.roblox.com/parents.

Questions to ask your child

  • "What's your favourite Roblox game at the moment? Can you show me how it works?"
  • "Who do you usually play with? Do you know them from school?"
  • "Has anyone ever offered you free Robux or asked you to talk somewhere else?"
  • "What would you do if someone made you feel weird in a game?"

What your child is actually doing

Your child picks a character (a "Brawler"), each with its own attack and super-move, and fights in short matches of about 2–3 minutes, usually 3 players against 3. The violence is cartoonish: characters "faint" and pop back up. The main modes include Gem Grab (collect and hold gems), Brawl Ball (football with attacks), Showdown (last one standing), Heist and Knockout.

A typical session: play a match, win or lose trophies, open rewards, upgrade a Brawler, and play again. Because each match is so short, "five more minutes" easily turns into an hour. Many children join a Club, a group with its own text chat, to play with others.

Why they love it: instant action, constant rewards, collecting Brawlers, and climbing the trophy ladder. It's also very popular among classmates, who compare Brawlers and ranks.

Checked weekly

Right now (November 2026)

  • New age rating: in October 2026, Europe's PEGI age-rating system began moving games with paid random items (loot-box style purchases) from PEGI 3, 7 or 12 up to PEGI 16. Brawl Stars was reported among the games affected. Nothing in the game itself changed: the new rating reflects the spending design, not violence.
  • Season 56, "Brawl Vice", a crime-city theme, started on 5 November.
  • Three new Brawlers: Thunder Frogs, Rey and Ruby. New Brawlers create strong pressure to unlock them quickly, and the fastest way is to pay.
  • Ranked mode was overhauled. It now unlocks at 5,000 trophies, so children may be playing a lot to "get to Ranked".
  • Coming in December: the "Brawlidays" holiday event with festive skins and limited-time offers.

What's good about it

  • No voice chat, and cartoon-level violence.
  • Quick strategic thinking and teamwork.
  • Easy to play together as a family, side by side.

The real risks

Time Most common

Short matches and constant rewards make it very hard to stop. Device-level time limits work better than willpower.

Spending Common

Gems, the Brawl Pass, new Brawlers and limited-time offers. Random rewards like Starr Drops build a "maybe this time" feeling. Most spending happens through the phone's app store, so that's where to block it.

Strangers in Clubs and chat Possible

Club chat lets children talk with players they don't know, and friend requests can follow random matches. German child-media experts flag a risk of cyberbullying and grooming here. Supercell does give under-16 accounts extra protection (see below).

Toxic behaviour and scams Common

Expect trash talk in chat, "free gems" websites and offers to buy or sell accounts. All of these are scams or against the rules, and they can lose your child their account.

Built-in protection for under-16s: Supercell's "Young Player" accounts don't show personalised offers or ads, use stricter chat filters, and only let children join Clubs labelled Family Friendly. Make sure your child's account has their real age.

Reduce the risk: the five things that matter most

  1. Turn on purchase approval on the phone or tablet (step-by-step below).
  2. Set a daily time limit for the app at device level.
  3. Set up a Supercell ID with a parent email so you can recover the account.
  4. Club with friends only: ideally a Club your child and their real friends set up.
  5. Agree a spending rule before the holiday event, e.g. "one Brawl Pass a term from pocket money".

Set up parental controls, step by step

Brawl Stars has few controls of its own, so the phone or tablet does most of the work.

iPhone / iPad (Screen Time)

  1. Settings › Screen Time › set up for your child, or use Family Sharing.
  2. Content & Privacy Restrictions › iTunes & App Store Purchases › In-app Purchases: Don't Allow. On the newest iOS versions this has moved to Screen Time › Apps and Websites › App Store › In-app Purchases: Blocked. Or turn on Ask to Buy in Family Sharing.
  3. App Limits › add a daily limit for Games, or for Brawl Stars specifically.

Android (Google Family Link / Play Store)

  1. Install Google Family Link on your phone and link your child's account.
  2. In the Play Store on the child's device: Profile › Settings › Purchase verification (called Authentication on some phones), then require verification for every purchase.
  3. In Family Link: set daily limits or app-specific time limits.

In the game

  1. Settings › Supercell ID › create one using a parent-controlled email address.
  2. Make sure the age entered is correct, so your child gets a Young Player account if under 16.
  3. Show your child how to mute, block and report players, and how to leave a Club.

Supercell is testing "Supercell ID Family", which lets parents monitor children's accounts. It can't limit time or block the game, so device controls are still needed. Supercell's guide: supercell.com/parents.

Questions to ask your child

  • "Which Brawler is your favourite, and why?"
  • "Are you in a Club? Who's in it?"
  • "What would you buy if you had gems? What makes it feel worth it?"
  • "How do you know when it's time to stop?"

What your child is actually doing

Minecraft is a world made of blocks that your child can dig, build and explore. The key question for parents is where they're playing, because the risk depends almost entirely on that:

WhereWhat it meansRisk
Single playerAlone in their own world.Very low

Two modes: Creative (unlimited blocks, just build) and Survival (gather resources and survive monsters at night).

Two editions: Bedrock (phones, tablets, consoles, Windows) and Java (PC/Mac, used for most mods and big independent servers). Ask which one your child plays.

Why they love it: total freedom to build anything, adventure, and playing with friends. Many also watch hours of Minecraft videos on YouTube.

Checked weekly

Right now (November 2026)

  • Minecraft now gets several smaller updates ("drops") a year. The autumn drop, Wilderness Bound (September), added a new forest with colourful poplar trees.
  • The winter drop, Deep Freeze, is due before the end of 2026. It adds ice caves and a new frozen monster, and children may already be testing it in preview versions.
  • Minecraft Dungeons II, a separate action-adventure game with no building, came out on 29 September. It may be on Christmas lists.
  • At school, students may use Minecraft Education, a separate, closed classroom version.

What's good about it

  • Creativity, planning, spatial reasoning, and even maths and coding ("redstone" circuits).
  • Can be played completely offline.
  • One of the easiest games for families to play together.

The real risks

Strangers on public servers Main risk

Chat on big servers exposes children to bad language, bullying and, rarely, grooming. As in other games, the danger escalates when someone moves the conversation to Discord, where many Minecraft communities have voice chat.

Unsafe downloads Common

Mods, "free Minecoins" and cheats from unofficial websites can carry viruses or steal accounts. Stick to the official Marketplace or well-known, trusted mod sites, ideally with a parent.

Spending Lower

Marketplace items and Realms subscriptions cost money, and some independent servers sell ranks or perks. The pressure is much lower than in Roblox or Fortnite.

Time and YouTube Manageable

Building projects absorb hours, and Minecraft YouTube can lead to less suitable channels. Set up YouTube parental controls too.

Reduce the risk: the five things that matter most

  1. Create a Microsoft family group with your child's account in it (this works even if you don't own an Xbox).
  2. Decide where they can play: offline and Realms for younger children; servers only once you've looked at them together.
  3. Chat: friends only, or off.
  4. Purchases: "ask a parent".
  5. No mods or downloads without you.

Set up parental controls, step by step

  1. Create a family group: go to family.microsoft.com, or install the Xbox Family Settings app, and sign in with your own Microsoft account. Add your child's Microsoft account, the one they use for Minecraft.
  2. Multiplayer: under your child › Privacy & online safety (or Online safety in the app) › You can join multiplayer games: Block, or Allow. Blocking stops servers, Realms and online worlds. Changes can take several hours, sometimes a day, to take effect.
  3. Communication: in the same section, set who can talk to your child with voice, text or invites to Friends, or block it.
  4. Friends: require approval for friend requests if your child is younger.
  5. Spending: turn on Ask a parent for purchases.
  6. Screen time: set daily limits per device in Microsoft Family Safety.
  7. Consoles: PlayStation and Nintendo Switch have their own parental controls too. Set those as well.
Choosing a server? Look for clear rules, active moderators and an allow-list (only approved players can join). Better still, pay for a Realm and invite only friends you both know.

Questions to ask your child

  • "Show me something you've built. How did you do it?"
  • "Do you play on your own world, a Realm, or a server? Which one?"
  • "Who else is on that server? Do you chat?"
  • "Have you ever downloaded anything for Minecraft? Where from?"

What your child is actually doing

Most children mean Battle Royale: up to 100 players drop onto an island, collect weapons and materials, and fight until one player or team is left. The violence is cartoonish, with no blood: defeated players simply vanish. Players can also build walls and ramps in seconds, a big part of the skill. Zero Build is the version without building.

But Fortnite now contains much more:

  • LEGO Fortnite: survival and building, similar to Minecraft. Gentler, and popular with younger children.
  • Fortnite Festival: a music and rhythm game.
  • Creative islands: thousands of games made by players, from obstacle courses to horror. Each island has its own age rating, which works much like Roblox.

A typical session: queue with friends (or random teammates), play a 20-minute match, complete daily challenges for Battle Pass rewards, check the Item Shop for new skins, and repeat.

Why they love it: playing with friends, the thrill of winning, skins, dances ("emotes") and big live events everyone talks about at school.

Checked weekly

Right now (November 2026)

  • Fortnite is in a short "mini-season", Treasure Hunters, which started on 1 November.
  • On 5 December, Epic has confirmed a live ending event for the current chapter and a new Battle Pass. A new map ("Chapter 8") is widely expected to follow. Expect children to be desperate to be online for the event, and to ask for the new Battle Pass.
  • Winterfest, the Christmas event with free gifts and seasonal skins, usually follows in mid-December.
  • Epic's parental controls include separate Play Time and Create Time limits, so you can limit playing and island-building separately.

What's good about it

  • Teamwork, quick decision-making and communication with friends.
  • LEGO Fortnite and Creative mode reward building and design.
  • Epic has some of the most detailed parental controls of any game.

The real risks

Voice chat with strangers Main risk

When your child plays with random teammates ("fill"), open voice chat can mean strangers' bad language, bullying or worse. Voice chat is off by default for under-18s, and younger children can't use it without parental consent. Check it hasn't been switched on. Console voice chat (PlayStation, Xbox) is separate and needs its own settings.

Spending and "fear of missing out" Very common

The Item Shop changes every day, and skins are status symbols at school. In 2022, Epic paid $520 million in US settlements over children's privacy and design tricks that led to unwanted purchases. Its systems are better now, but the pressure to buy remains.

Player-made islands Varies

Creative islands range from gentle to frightening. Use the content rating setting to control which islands your child can open.

Scams Common

"Free V-Bucks" websites and videos are always scams. They aim to steal accounts or personal details.

Time and sleep Common

Matches can't be paused, and live events sometimes happen late in the evening in Swedish time.

Automatic protection for younger children: if your child is under 13, Epic creates a Cabined Account. Voice chat, free text chat and real-money purchases are off until a parent gives consent and sets up controls. If your child says "it needs parent permission", that's this, and it's a good moment to set things up properly.

Reduce the risk: the five things that matter most

  1. Set up Epic parental controls with a 6-digit PIN that only you know.
  2. Voice chat: friends only, or nobody. Check the console settings too.
  3. Purchases need the PIN, and paid random items are off.
  4. Island content rating set to match your child's age.
  5. Play Time limits, plus agreed exceptions for big events.

Set up parental controls, step by step

  1. Sign in to your child's Epic account on the Epic Games website (easiest on a computer), or open Fortnite › menu › Settings (gear) › Parental Controls.
  2. Confirm a parent email and create a 6-digit PIN. The PIN locks every setting.
  3. Voice Chat Permission: Nobody or Friends Only is best for most children. For under-10s, Friends Only is the most open option Epic allows; for under-13s it's Friends, team members and connections. Avoid "Everybody".
  4. Text Chat Permission: same choices. Turn on Filter Mature Language.
  5. Require a PIN to add friends.
  6. Content ratings: set the highest island rating your child can open without your PIN.
  7. Purchases: require the PIN for real-money purchases, and switch off Allow Paid Random Items.
  8. Time limits: set daily Play Time (and Create Time) limits, and turn on weekly playtime reports by email.
  9. Console: set chat and spending limits in the PlayStation, Xbox or Nintendo parental controls too.

Epic's guide: safety.epicgames.com/parental-controls. These controls also cover Rocket League and Fall Guys.

Find out what your child is playing

Many parents don't know exactly which games their child plays, or which games inside a game. That's normal. Here's how to find out without turning it into an interrogation.

1. Ask them to teach you

Children love being the expert. "Can you show me your favourite game and teach me how to play?" tells you more in ten minutes than any setting. Watch for: who they play with, whether there's chat, and what the game asks them to buy.

Good conversation starters

  • "What are people in your class playing at the moment?"
  • "What's the best thing that happened in a game this week?"
  • "Is there anyone in your games who isn't from school?"
  • "Has anything ever happened in a game that you didn't like?"
  • "If you could buy one thing in a game, what would it be?"

Tip: car journeys and side-by-side activities work better than face-to-face questioning.

2. Check the devices

DeviceWhere to look
iPhone / iPadSettings › Screen Time › See All App & Website Activity shows which apps are used and for how long.
AndroidGoogle Family Link app on your phone › your child › app activity.
Xbox / MinecraftXbox Family Settings app › activity reports.

PlayStationFamily Management in your PlayStation account › play time.
RobloxLinked parent account › your child's most-played games and screen time.
FortniteEpic parental controls › weekly playtime report by email.

3. Know the "hidden" apps

The biggest risks often aren't in the game but in the apps used alongside it. If you see any of these, ask about them:

  • Discord: voice and text chat servers used by gamers (13+). Many game communities live here.
  • Snapchat, WhatsApp, Telegram: where strangers try to move conversations.
  • YouTube and TikTok: game videos, which can lead to adult content and "free currency" scams.

4. Look out for these signs

Worth a gentle chat

  • A new online "friend" who gives gifts or currency.
  • Secrecy, or switching screens quickly.
  • Playing late at night.
  • Unexplained purchases or in-game items.
  • Upset or withdrawn after playing.

Act now

  • Requests for photos or to keep secrets.
  • Pressure to move to another app.
  • Threats, or demands for money or images.
  • An adult your child won't talk about.

Get help

A child is in immediate danger? Call 112.

What to do first

  1. Stay calm and thank your child for telling you. How you react decides whether they tell you next time.
  2. Don't delete anything. Screenshot usernames, messages and dates.
  3. Report and block the user in the game.
  4. Contact the police if there is any sexual content, request for images, threat or demand for money.
  5. Tell the school so staff can support your child.
